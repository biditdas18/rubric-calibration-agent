% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Annotation quality is a limiting factor in supervised learning pipelines
that rely on the LLM-as-judge paradigm. This paper's central finding is that
automated inter-judge convergence is \emph{not sufficient evidence of
alignment with human judgment}---we demonstrate this four ways, on the same
underlying corpus and (for three of the four) the same 30-item human
reference set, so these should be read as related diagnostic analyses
rather than fully independent replications: a human validation study
showing one domain's high inter-judge agreement masked a systematic
divergence from human judgment; a frontier-model baseline showing a
calibrated rubric does not transfer to a different judge model; a
reasoning-before-label ablation showing explicit reasoning can raise
inter-judge agreement without raising agreement with human judgment; and a
judge-replacement test on the one local judge diagnosed as the bottleneck,
which raised inter-judge agreement substantially (0.700\,$\to$\,0.883) by
converting near-unanimous dissent into full unanimity, while leaving the
panel's majority-vote output---and its accuracy against human-labeled
transcripts---bit-for-bit identical: the two retained judges already
agreed with each other on 90\% of this domain's transcripts, so the third
judge could not have changed the majority label on this set regardless of
which model it was (Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}). We reach this
finding while building and evaluating \textbf{CRUCIBLE}, a multi-agent
system that automatically calibrates annotation rubrics through iterative
disagreement diagnosis and targeted revision. Manual rubric refinement
does not scale and introduces annotator-specific bias that usually goes
unaudited and is hard to reproduce. Three heterogeneous local judge models
independently evaluate a transcript corpus; a coordinator model (Claude
Sonnet) analyzes per-model criterion-level disagreement patterns and
proposes rubric revisions; the loop repeats until all domains converge or a
fixed iteration budget is exhausted, flagging any domain whose agreement
stagnates. CRUCIBLE was developed to solve a concrete annotation bottleneck
in building a Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) classifier for YouTube educational
content, motivated by an earlier, differently-scoped pilot (a frontier
chat-model ensemble applying a domain-agnostic rubric, reported in the
companion SNR paper) that showed substantial, inconsistent inter-judge
disagreement. Applied to 60 YouTube transcripts across three content
domains---General Education, Technology \& AI, and Career \&
Self-Improvement---CRUCIBLE reached 96.7\% and 81.7\% weighted inter-judge
agreement on two domains, while the third domain failed to converge, its
best rubric reaching only 70.0\%, near the floor of the weighted metric. We
initially read this as a pure model-capability ceiling; our later
reasoning-before-label and human-validation analyses instead suggest a
shared misalignment between judge models and human annotators over what
counts as ``substantive'' career content plausibly contributes alongside
any capability limit of the judge models in isolation---though, per the
judge-replacement finding above, we cannot rule out that the ceiling on
this specific panel was partly a structural artifact of two judges already
agreeing with each other. Because raw agreement is inflated by label
prevalence, we report chance-corrected agreement (Fleiss' $\kappa$ and
Gwet's AC1), which are less affected by the prevalence paradox under these
marginals than raw agreement; these---read alongside the human validation
study---locate CRUCIBLE's value less in raising raw agreement than in
diagnosing whether residual disagreement is rubric-fixable,
capability-bounded, or prevalence-dominated, and in showing when that
automated diagnosis itself needs an external check. We additionally check
whether the calibrated rubrics' effect on agreement generalizes to a
90-transcript corpus never seen by the coordinator during revision; the
weighted-agreement metric improves there too, though the underlying
majority-vote annotations themselves do not change on any of the 90
transcripts (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}; see Limitations for the precise
sense in which this corpus is unseen). A pre-run discriminative-validity
screening check identified a degenerate judge (Gemma 2 9B, producing
identical LOW labels for all 60 transcripts) during development, before
the calibration run reported here; it was replaced with Qwen 2.5 7B. The
calibrated rubrics, applied by a separate frontier ensemble (reported in
the companion paper), produced labels that trained a binary classifier on
the SNR task achieving F1\,=\,0.871 with recall\,=\,0.964 on the
HIGH-signal class as a downstream case study; we separately find that this
same frontier ensemble tracks human judgment about as loosely as the local
calibration panel does. Code and calibration logs are available at
\url{https://github.com/biditdas18/rubric-calibration-agent}.
\end{abstract}

% ── 1. Introduction ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

The LLM-as-judge paradigm has become a standard component of annotation
pipelines in settings where human labeling is expensive, slow, or subject to
individual bias~\cite{zheng2023judging}. A single LLM prompted with an
evaluation rubric can produce labels at scale, but single-model annotation
carries systematic biases rooted in the model's training distribution,
instruction-following style, and boundary-case
interpretation~\cite{shi2024judging}. Multi-model ensembles reduce this risk,
but introduce a new dependency: the rubric must be precise enough that multiple
independent models reach consistent conclusions. A poorly specified rubric
produces disagreement not because the content is genuinely ambiguous, but
because different models resolve underspecified criteria differently.

This paper originates from a concrete failure of that kind. We were building a
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) framework\footnote{The SNR classifier is described
in a companion paper: Das, B.
(2026). Signal-to-Noise Ratio Detection in Educational Video Content: A
Binary Classification Framework Using Transcript Embeddings. SSRN.
\url{https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6866745}. That
work cites CRUCIBLE as its annotation methodology.} for binary classification of YouTube educational video
transcripts---separating substantive instructional content (HIGH signal:
concrete actionable steps, methodological coherence, named frameworks) from
low-value filler (LOW signal: generic motivational advice, fear-based framing,
promotional material, repetition without progression). The task required
labeled training data reliable enough to train a lightweight classifier
deployable at inference scale.

During development, an earlier annotation pipeline---a differently-scoped,
differently-modeled experiment reported in the companion SNR
paper~\cite{dasSNR2026}---showed substantial and inconsistent disagreement
across judges, motivating the need for systematic rubric
diagnostics.\footnote{That pilot used a domain-agnostic rubric applied
across a frontier chat-model ensemble (GPT-4, Gemini, Meta AI), distinct
from the local judge panel (Llama 3.1 8B, Mistral 7B, Qwen 2.5 7B) used for
calibration in this paper, and a 3-category (HIGH/MID/LOW) label space
rather than the binary space CRUCIBLE's own weighted-$A$ metric
(Eq.~\ref{eq:agreement}) operates in. See Appendix~\ref{app:baseline} for the exact figures and why
they are not directly comparable in magnitude to CRUCIBLE's own convergence
numbers.}
Two remedies were available: (1)~manually label a gold dataset per domain, or
(2)~automatically calibrate the rubrics until the ensemble converges.
Option~1 was rejected on two grounds. First, reviewing transcripts of 10--15
minute videos at the scale required is prohibitively time-intensive. Second,
and more fundamentally, manual annotation introduces annotator-specific bias that is reducible only through
multiple annotators and adjudication:
an annotator who values career development content intrinsically applies a
systematically different threshold than one who does not. Without a multi-annotator comparison, that bias goes unaudited and undisclosed---baked into every label silently.

We designed CRUCIBLE to solve this. It is a coordinator-mediated, iterative
calibration loop requiring no human reference labels and no domain expertise
beyond an initial rubric draft. Three heterogeneous local models serve as
judges; a frontier coordinator model diagnoses their disagreement patterns and
proposes targeted rubric revisions; the loop repeats until convergence or a fixed iteration budget is exhausted. The resulting domain-specific rubrics are versioned and auditable.
The labels they produce are silver quality---not gold---but empirically
validated against a measurable inter-judge criterion rather than a single
annotator's unchecked judgment.

The downstream classifier serves as a case study in whether the calibrated
labels are usable at scale, not as an independent validation of their
agreement with the available human reference (Section~\ref{sec:results}
and Limitations address that question directly). The rubrics produced by
CRUCIBLE were applied by a
separate frontier ensemble (GPT-4o mini, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Llama 3.3 70B;
reported in the companion paper) to label transcripts; an SVM trained on
389 transcripts (89 real, labeled via this calibrated-rubric ensemble
methodology, plus 300 synthetically generated transcripts with labels
assigned by construction, intended to match the generating prompt's target
class) achieved F1\,=\,0.871 with recall\,=\,0.964 on the
HIGH-signal class, evaluated on a disjoint held-out set of 60 real
transcripts, compared to a majority-class baseline of F1\,=\,0.000 and, in
a synthetic-only training ablation reported in the companion
paper~\cite{dasSNR2026}, F1\,=\,0.747 when trained on the 300 synthetic
transcripts alone---indicating the real, calibrated-rubric-labeled
transcripts contribute materially beyond what synthetic data alone
provides. We did not attempt training on labels from the uncalibrated pilot
pipeline described above; the majority-class baseline (F1\,=\,0.000)
indicates the task itself is non-trivial.

Beyond the calibration loop and this downstream case study, a set of
follow-up analyses---some conducted after the initial calibration run,
one (judge replacement, Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}) run as a
direct, controlled test of this paper's own prescriptive claim---surfaced
this paper's central finding: CRUCIBLE's own convergence signal is not by
itself sufficient evidence of label quality. This paper is organized around
two questions those analyses answer: \textbf{(1)} does disagreement-driven
rubric revision generalize past the transcripts it was tuned on, and
\textbf{(2)} does higher inter-judge agreement correspond to higher
agreement with human judgment. An external human validation study, a
frontier-model rubric-transfer test, a reasoning-before-label ablation, and
a direct judge-replacement test each speak to question (2); an evaluation
on a 90-transcript corpus unseen by the coordinator during revision speaks
to question (1). We report these alongside the calibration results because
they qualify how those results should be read, and we return to both
questions explicitly in Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:discussion}.

\textbf{The core contributions of this paper are:}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=\arabic*.]

\item \textbf{Automated inter-judge convergence is not sufficient evidence
of alignment with human judgment.} We demonstrate this four ways: a
human validation study (Section~\ref{sec:humanval}) shows Technology \& AI's
high inter-judge agreement (81.7\%) masked a systematic divergence from
human judgment; a frontier-model baseline (Section~\ref{sec:portability})
shows the calibrated rubric does not transfer to a different judge model,
instead teaching it to mimic the local ensemble's specific errors rather
than improving its agreement with human judgment; a reasoning-before-label
ablation (Section~\ref{sec:cotresults}) shows explicit reasoning can raise
inter-judge agreement without raising agreement with human judgment; and a
judge-replacement test (Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}) raises
inter-judge agreement substantially (0.700\,$\to$\,0.883) while leaving the
panel's majority-vote output---and its agreement with human-labeled
transcripts---completely unchanged, since the two retained judges already
agreed with each other on 90\% of this domain's transcripts, so the third
judge could not have changed the majority label regardless of which model
it was; this fourth result is narrower than the raw agreement numbers
alone suggest (Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement} discusses the
implications). The first three share a common 30-item human-reference set
and are related diagnostic analyses rather than independent replications;
the fourth is a single controlled trial on the same reference set.
Together they show agreement metrics can be high, or can be made to rise,
while agreement with the available human reference is not.

\item \textbf{An automated rubric calibration loop} with coordinator-mediated
disagreement diagnosis and targeted revision. Unlike prior rubric-generation and refinement systems, which are validated against
reference judgments or accuracy signals, CRUCIBLE studies a narrower setting: iterative
rubric revision driven solely by inter-judge disagreement, with no human reference labels used during calibration.

\item \textbf{A held-out generalization check.} Re-scoring both the initial
and final calibrated rubrics on a 90-transcript corpus never shown to the
coordinator during revision (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}) shows the revision's
effect on the weighted inter-judge \emph{agreement metric} generalizes past
the calibration corpus for the two domains that were actually revised, with
a positive, CI-excluding-zero effect for Career \& Self-Improvement
specifically---though, as detailed there, this generalization is driven
disproportionately by one judge's behavior shifting, not uniform three-way
convergence, and the underlying majority-vote annotations do not change on
any of the 90 transcripts under either rubric.

\item \textbf{The capability-ceiling finding, revised.} The system
empirically distinguishes rubric-fixable disagreement (Technology \& AI:
76.7\% $\rightarrow$ 81.7\% in one iteration) from disagreement that further
rubric revision did not resolve (Career \& Self-Improvement: failed to
converge despite two revisions, with its best rubric at 70.0\%). We
initially attributed this to a pure model-capability limitation; the
reasoning-before-label ablation and human validation study
(Sections~\ref{sec:cotresults}~and~\ref{sec:humanval}) suggest the ceiling
may instead partly reflect a shared misalignment between judge models and
human annotators over what counts as ``substantive'' career content, though
we cannot fully separate this from a structural property of the panel
itself: our judge-replacement test (Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement})
found the two static judges already agreed with each other on 90\% of this
domain's transcripts, so no third judge could have changed the panel's
majority-vote output on this set regardless of the ceiling's underlying
cause.

\item \textbf{Per-domain convergence.} Domains converge independently, each
against its own rubric and disagreement history. Two supporting
quality-control components are detailed in
Sections~\ref{sec:judgeqc}--\ref{sec:columnar}: a discriminative-validity
pre-screening gate that caught and removed a degenerate judge (Gemma~2~9B,
zero discriminative outputs) before the reported run, and a columnar
evaluation order that made repeated 60-transcript, 3-model passes tractable
on consumer hardware. Each iteration also re-evaluates any domain that
converged on the immediately preceding iteration, as a one-time check that
its agreement rate holds on a second pass with the same rubric and judges
(Section~\ref{sec:regressioncheck})---an output-stability check, not a
cross-domain interference test, since coordinator calls are single-domain,
stateless API requests with no mechanism for one domain's revision to
affect another's.

\item \textbf{A downstream case study.} The calibrated rubrics, applied by a
separate frontier ensemble, produced labels that trained an SVM classifier
achieving F1\,=\,0.871 and recall\,=\,0.964 on the HIGH-signal class
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). We treat this as a usability case study, not an
independent validation of the labels' agreement with the available human
reference: Section~\ref{sec:frontierhuman} shows the same frontier
ensemble tracks human judgment about as loosely as the local calibration
panel does.

\end{enumerate}

% ── 2. Related Work ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{LLM-as-Judge}

The use of large language models as automated evaluators was formalized by
Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2023judging} in the MT-Bench benchmark. Subsequent
work extended this paradigm to structured rubrics~\cite{liu2023geval},
fine-grained multi-dimensional scoring~\cite{kim2024prometheus}, and
customizable evaluation dimensions~\cite{fu2023gptscore}. A persistent finding is
that single-model judges exhibit systematic biases---position bias, verbosity
bias, and self-preference---that reduce
reliability~\cite{zheng2023judging, shi2024judging}. Multi-model ensembles have been proposed to
mitigate these biases~\cite{chan2023chateval}, but ensemble reliability still
depends on rubric quality.

\subsection{Rubric Design and Calibration}

LLM-Rubric~\cite{hashemi2024llmrubric} combines LLM predictions with a small
neural network to predict human judge annotations. AdaRubric~\cite{adarubric2026}
proposes task-adaptive rubric generation for agent evaluation.
Autorubric~\cite{autorubric2026} provides a unifying framework for rubric-based
evaluation across criterion types. Prior
rubric-refinement work that does iterate does so against human-labeled reference
data~\cite{patch2025rubric}. Our narrower claim relative to this body of
work is not that automated rubric revision itself is unaddressed in
general, but that we revise domain-specific annotation rubrics using
cross-model disagreement as the sole feedback signal, with no human reference labels used during calibration, while explicitly investigating when that
signal produces panel-specific consensus rather than alignment with human
judgment (Sections~\ref{sec:humanval}--\ref{sec:cotresults}) --- a question
the reference-judgment-validated systems above are not positioned to ask,
since their validation signal is human-labeled by construction.
SciIF~\cite{sciif2026} pairs problems with a fixed catalog of scientific constraints and structured
verification rubrics, but treats the rubric as fixed rather than something to be revised.
Recent work also automates rubric generation and refinement: RRD~\cite{shen2026rrd} recursively
decomposes and filters rubric criteria, and GER-Eval~\cite{siro2026ger} has LLMs design and apply their
own rubrics. Both are still validated against reference judgments or accuracy signals; CRUCIBLE instead
drives revision purely from inter-judge agreement with no human reference labels, and separately
audits, via human validation, when that agreement-only signal is and is not trustworthy.

\subsection{Disagreement Diagnosis}

Related self-deliberation mechanisms appear in evaluation frameworks such as
AraTable~\cite{aratable2025}, where a judge re-examines its own
assessment. Multi-agent debate~\cite{du2023improving} resolves disagreement through structured exchange
among models. These approaches are self-revision or debate; neither produces a generalized rubric revision that
improves agreement across the full corpus and on future evaluation runs.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Coordination}

Multi-agent debate frameworks~\cite{du2023improving} have been applied to
improve LLM output reliability through structured disagreement resolution.
CRUCIBLE applies the coordinator-mediator pattern asymmetrically: judge models
do not communicate with each other; only the coordinator sees aggregate
disagreement patterns and produces the revision.

\subsection{Annotation Bias and Scalability}

The annotation bottleneck has been addressed through weak
supervision~\cite{ratner2017snorkel}, programmatic
labeling~\cite{bach2019snorkel}, and knowledge distillation from frontier
models~\cite{hinton2015distilling}. CRUCIBLE is complementary: it produces
rubrics calibrated against inter-judge agreement, grounding ensemble
annotation at scale---though, as Section~\ref{sec:humanval} shows, that
calibration signal alone does not guarantee the rubrics are human-validated.

% ── 3. The CRUCIBLE System ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{The CRUCIBLE System}

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Given a corpus $\mathcal{D} = \{d_1, \ldots, d_N\}$ to be annotated with a
binary label $y \in \{H, L\}$, and an initial rubric $R^{(0)}$ specifying
criteria for each label, we seek a revised rubric $R^{(k)}$ such that the
weighted inter-judge agreement $A(R^{(k)}, \mathcal{D}) \geq \tau$ for a
target threshold $\tau$, with the revision process requiring no human-labeled
reference data.

The key insight is that inter-judge disagreement encodes rubric
underspecification: when two competent models applying the same rubric reach
opposite conclusions on the same document, the rubric has not resolved the
case. A coordinator with visibility into both decisions and the criterion-level
justifications can identify \emph{which criterion was misapplied and
how}---and propose a targeted revision to close the gap.

independently and in isolation from each other; only the coordinator sees their
combined outputs. The rubric revision loop is the only feedback path---there
is no human in the loop after initialization.

\subsection{System Components}

\textbf{Judge models.} Three heterogeneous local models running at
temperature~0.0 via Ollama: Llama~3.1~8B (Meta), Mistral~7B (Mistral AI), and
Qwen~2.5~7B (Alibaba). Temperature~0.0 yields near-deterministic outputs, isolating
rubric quality as the variable of interest. The three models were chosen to
represent different training lineages, maximizing the diagnostic value of their
disagreements.
These local models serve as the calibration instrument only; production labeling in the
downstream application uses a separate, stronger frontier ensemble (companion paper).

\textbf{Coordinator model.} Claude Sonnet (claude-sonnet-4-6)~\cite{anthropicClaudeSonnet46}, via API. The
coordinator receives: (a)~the current rubric, (b)~per-model label assignments
and criterion-level justification quotes for each disagreement case, and
(c)~disagreement counts per criterion. It produces a structured JSON revision
proposal specifying which criterion to modify and the revised text.

\textbf{Agreement metric.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:agreement}
  A = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i, \qquad
  w_i = \begin{cases}
    1.0   & \text{all three judges agree} \\
    0.667 & \text{exactly two judges agree}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For three judges on a binary label, every item is either unanimous ($w=1.0$) or a
two--one majority ($w=0.667$); the no-majority case cannot occur, so $A$ is bounded below
by $0.667$. Under independent judges the expected value of $A$ is approximately $0.75$,
which should be read as the chance level for this metric; label skew can raise it further.

All-agree rate (unanimous three-way agreement) is tracked separately as a
stricter secondary metric.

\subsection{Calibration Loop}
\label{sec:calibrationloop}

\textbf{Initialization.} Domain-specific rubrics $R_d^{(0)}$ are written at
moderate abstraction---sufficient to convey intent but not yet precise enough
to resolve edge cases consistently.

\textbf{Iteration $k$:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
  \item All three judges evaluate all documents using $\{R_d^{(k)}\}$.
  \item Weighted agreement $A_d^{(k)}$ is computed per domain.
  \item For each non-converged, non-stagnated domain with $A_d^{(k)} < \tau$:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*., noitemsep]
      \item Extract disagreement cases (at least one judge diverged).
      \item Assemble per-model criterion quotes for each case.
      \item Coordinator diagnoses and proposes revision.
      \item If valid JSON: $R_d^{(k+1)} \leftarrow \mathrm{revise}(R_d^{(k)})$.
      \item Irreducible cases (genuine ambiguity, not rubric failure) are logged
            and excluded from stagnation counting.
    \end{enumerate}
  \item Regression check on each already-converged domain.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Stopping.} A domain is marked \emph{stagnated} when $\Delta A_d \leq 0$ for three
consecutive iterations; it is then excluded from further revision and held at its current
rubric, but this does not terminate the loop. The loop terminates only when (a)~all domains
have converged or (b)~the maximum of 10 iterations is reached. Because Career \&
Self-Improvement never converged, the present run continued to the 10-iteration cap, with
converged domains monitored by regression checks and the stagnated domain held in place.

\subsection{Judge Quality Control}
\label{sec:judgeqc}

Prior to the primary run, each candidate judge is evaluated for discriminative
validity. A degenerate judge---one assigning a single label to all
documents---provides zero inter-judge signal and pollutes agreement scores.

During development, Gemma~2 9B produced LOW labels for all 60 transcripts
across all domains (0\% HIGH rate). This is consistent with treating LOW
criteria as a checklist to confirm rather than a comparative threshold to apply.
Gemma~2 9B was replaced with Qwen~2.5~7B before the calibration run reported here.

\subsection{Columnar Evaluation Order: An Engineering Optimization}
\label{sec:columnar}

The order in which judge models are invoked across the corpus has a
dramatic effect on runtime: batching evaluation by model rather than by
document (running each judge across all 60 transcripts before switching
models) reduces model-load events from 180 to 3 per iteration and cuts
per-iteration runtime from an incomplete $>$30 minutes to 17--20 minutes on
consumer hardware, making the full 10-iteration run tractable. This is an
engineering detail, not a scientific finding; the full derivation and the
naive row-wise alternative it replaces are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:columnar} for readers interested in reproducing the
setup.

% ── 4. Experimental Setup ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimentalsetup}

\textbf{Task and corpus.} The target task is binary classification of YouTube
educational video transcripts under the SNR taxonomy: HIGH signal (substantive
instructional content: concrete actionable steps, methodological coherence,
named frameworks) vs.\ LOW signal (generic motivational advice, fear-based
framing, promotional material, repetition without progression). The corpus
comprises 60 transcripts across three domains (20 per domain): General
Education, Technology \& AI, and Career \& Self-Improvement.

\textbf{Sampling protocol.} Transcripts were collected via two methods:
approximately 20 via manual search, and the remaining approximately 130
(spanning both this 60-transcript evaluation corpus and the 90-transcript
training-supplement corpus used in Section~\ref{sec:heldout}) via the
YouTube Data API v3, using a VPN to work around geographic and rate-limit
restrictions. API search was constrained to medium-length videos
(4--20 minutes) with closed captions available, filtered for English
relevance, using domain-specific query sets (5 base queries per domain,
expanded with ``tips''/``tutorial'' variants when needed to fill the
candidate pool); candidates were deduplicated against the existing
evaluation set and filtered to 300--8000 words per transcript. Candidate
selection prioritized a fixed allow-list of known channels per domain
(established educational/technical channels expected to produce clear HIGH
signal) before falling back to general search results---a
deliberate design choice trading naturalistic diversity for more
consistent signal quality, and a limitation on how representative the
corpus is of YouTube educational content generally (see Limitations). This
allow-list bias plausibly contributes to the strong HIGH-signal skew
reported below (\textbf{Corpus label distribution}): a channel allow-list
built to surface clear HIGH-signal content is, by construction, unlikely
to also surface a representative share of genuinely LOW-signal content, so
the corpus is better described as a curated educational-content sample
than as representative of YouTube educational content generally.

\textbf{Corpus label distribution.} Both corpora are strongly HIGH-skewed
at the majority-vote level. On the 60-transcript evaluation corpus (final
calibrated labels), majority-vote HIGH rate is 100\% for General Education,
60\% for Technology \& AI, and 90\% for Career \& Self-Improvement; the
individual judges are more HIGH-skewed still (Llama~3.1~8B: 100\%, 85\%,
100\% HIGH by domain; Mistral~7B: 100\%, 60\%, 90\%; Qwen~2.5~7B, the more
LOW-leaning judge: 90\%, 30\%, 10\%). On the 90-transcript held-out corpus
(final rubric), majority-vote HIGH rate is 100\% in every domain (Llama and
Mistral predict HIGH on essentially every transcript in both corpora;
Qwen~2.5~7B is again the only judge with substantial LOW-rate mass:
3\%, 37\%, 63\% HIGH by domain under the final rubric). We report these
figures because they bear directly on how several results in this paper
should be read---most importantly Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}'s
judge-replacement test, where Llama and Mistral's near-total mutual
agreement on Career \& Self-Improvement leaves little room for a third
judge to influence the panel's majority-vote output (Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}
quantifies this directly).

\textbf{Channel coverage.} The 60-transcript evaluation corpus spans 46
unique channels (up to 5 transcripts from a single channel); the
90-transcript training-supplement corpus (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}) spans
50 unique channels (up to 7 from a single channel). The two corpora share 6
channels in common. Consequently, ``held-out'' in
Section~\ref{sec:heldout} means \emph{document}-held-out (no transcript
appears in both corpora), not \emph{source}-held-out---see Limitations.

\textbf{Initial rubrics.} Three domain-specific rubrics at version~0, each
specifying HIGH-signal criteria (methodological coherence, specificity,
informational novelty, solution orientation) and LOW-signal patterns (generic
advice, repetition, fear-based framing, promotional content, logical fallacies)
with domain-appropriate threshold language.

\textbf{Target threshold.} $\tau = 0.80$. This is a modest margin above the
metric's chance level of $\approx 0.75$ under independent judges
(Eq.~\ref{eq:agreement}), not a claim that $0.80$ represents strong
agreement. This $\approx 0.75$ chance level is derived entirely from
CRUCIBLE's own binary weighted-$A$ metric and local judge panel; it is
unrelated to, and should not be blended with, the separate 3-category
pilot discussed in the Introduction and Appendix~\ref{app:baseline}, which
concerns a different experiment in a different label space. We treat
$\tau$ as a practical stopping rule for the calibration
loop---a minimum bar past which further iteration is not obviously
worthwhile---rather than as the paper's primary quality signal; the
chance-corrected metrics (Fleiss' $\kappa$, Gwet's AC1) reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results} are what we rely on to judge whether agreement
reflects genuine convergence.

\textbf{Coordinator retry.} Up to 3 retries per call on JSON parsing failure;
5-second delay between attempts.

\textbf{Runtime.} 17--20 minutes per iteration (columnar order). 170.7 minutes
total; the run terminated at the maximum-iteration cap of 10 iterations.
Consumer MacBook (Apple Silicon), no discrete GPU; exact CPU/GPU offload
configuration for the Ollama-served judge models was not recorded, and
Ollama's default behavior on Apple Silicon is to use Metal GPU
acceleration where available, so ``CPU-only'' should not be assumed.

\textbf{Baseline.} CRUCIBLE's own domain-specific v0 rubrics are the
calibration starting point (Table~\ref{tab:results}). The earlier,
differently-scoped pilot mentioned in the Introduction (frontier chat-model
ensemble, domain-agnostic rubric, 3-category label space) motivated this
work but is not a directly comparable quantitative baseline for the
figures below; see Appendix~\ref{app:baseline}.

capability-bounded, or prevalence-dominated.

to the final result; the revision loop contributed nothing because none
was needed. In Technology \& AI, the domain-specific v0 rubric started at
76.7\%, and one revision then added 5.0 points to cross threshold at
81.7\%. In Career \& Self-Improvement, the domain-specific v0 rubric
started at 68.4\%; one revision (v1) added a further 1.6 points to 70.0\%
before a second revision (v2) regressed performance and was discarded.
Across all three domains, the domain-specific v0 rubric alone accounts for
most of each domain's final agreement level; the iterative loop's marginal
contribution is small in absolute terms, necessary to cross threshold in
Technology \& AI, and attempted-but-unhelpful in Career \&
Self-Improvement---itself informative, as it is what let us distinguish a
rubric-fixable domain from one exhibiting the capability-ceiling pattern
investigated further in Section~\ref{sec:cotresults} and directly tested
via judge replacement in Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}.

mechanically fixed regardless of Qwen's individual prediction: the
revision shifted the dissenting judge's individual predictions but changed
the ensemble's \emph{majority-vote annotation} on \textbf{0 of the 90}
held-out transcripts (0 of 30 in each domain) between the v0 and final
rubric. The weighted-agreement improvement reported above reflects Qwen's
predictions moving closer to unanimity with an already-fixed majority, not
a change in which label the panel actually output on any transcript. Full
per-judge prevalence and provenance detail are in Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}.

\textbf{Train/test split and label independence.} The classifier is trained
on 389 transcripts: 89 real transcripts with silver labels assigned via the
same calibrated-rubric ensemble methodology, combined with 300 synthetic
transcripts (150 HIGH, 150 LOW, balanced across domains) generated from
structured prompts with labels assigned by construction, intended to match
the generating prompt's target class. Evaluation uses a
disjoint, held-out set of 60 real transcripts---the same 60-transcript
corpus used throughout this paper---selected and labeled prior to any
classifier training. The 89 training-supplement transcripts and 300
synthetic transcripts are disjoint from the 60-transcript evaluation set by
construction (no transcript appears in both); synthetic transcripts
correspond to no real video, and the 89 real training-supplement
transcripts are document-disjoint from evaluation but---per
Section~\ref{sec:experimentalsetup}---share some channels with it (6 of 46
evaluation-set channels also appear in the 90-transcript training-supplement
pool from which the 89 are drawn), so this is document-held-out, not
source-held-out. We note directly, per the concern motivating
Section~\ref{sec:humanval}, that
the evaluation labels are \emph{not} independent of the calibration process
in the sense of an external human reference---they are assigned by the same
class of frontier-ensemble judges used for the training supplement, applying the
calibrated rubric. This is precisely the circularity Section~\ref{sec:humanval}
is designed to address: human consensus and calibrated-panel labels agree
at 90\% (General Education), 50\% (Technology \& AI), and 50\% (Career \&
Self-Improvement)---figures that should be read alongside, and as a caveat
on, the F1 reported above. The F1 score demonstrates the calibrated labels
are \emph{learnable} by a downstream classifier; it does not by itself
demonstrate the labels are \emph{correct}.

\subsection{Human Validation}
\label{sec:humanval}

A known limitation of LLM-as-judge pipelines is that inter-judge agreement
among automated annotators may reflect shared model bias rather than
agreement with independent human judgment, and without a human reference
point, convergence cannot be distinguished from consensus error. We
address this directly with an independent human validation study.

\subsubsection{Design}

We recruited three independent annotators per domain (nine total) via
Prolific~\cite{palan2018prolific}, stratified by domain (10 transcripts per
domain, sampled from the 60-transcript corpus, plus one hidden
attention-check item per domain not included in the analysis below).
Annotators were required to be proficient in English and hold a Prolific
approval rating above 95\%. Annotators were shown only the raw transcript
text---not the calibrated rubric, not the domain label, and not CRUCIBLE's
output---and asked to judge, in their own words, whether each transcript
was substantive and valuable or generic and low-value, with a mandatory
written justification. This is an unaided-judgment design only; we did not
run a second condition showing annotators the calibrated rubric (see
Limitations for what this does and does not establish). This design is
deliberate: showing annotators the calibrated rubric would test whether
humans can \emph{follow} CRUCIBLE's criteria, not whether CRUCIBLE's
criteria independently match human judgment. Quality was enforced via a
hidden attention-check item per domain (adversarial or unambiguous content
with a known expected label), a mandatory content-grounded justification,
and task instructions designed to flag fast or low-effort responses for
rejection. One of nine submissions (Technology \& AI domain) was rejected
and replaced, on three converging factors confirmed by reviewing the full
submission: a completion time of 39:06 against this batch's displayed
median of 140:00, failure of the domain's attention-check item, and
justifications consisting largely of copy-pasted transcript excerpts
rather than substantive reasoning. This was a multi-factor judgment made
after reviewing the submission's full content, not a blind,
mechanically-triggered rejection; we report a sensitivity check in
Appendix~\ref{app:prolific} showing the domain-level results are unchanged
whether this submission or its replacement is used.

\subsubsection{Results}

We report two quantities per domain: (1)~inter-annotator agreement among
the three humans, via Fleiss' $\kappa$ and Gwet's AC1~\cite{gwet2008}, and
(2)~the agreement between human majority-vote consensus and CRUCIBLE's
calibrated label for the same transcript, with 95\% bootstrap confidence
intervals (10{,}000 resamples) given the small per-domain sample. With
$n=10$ per domain, percentile bootstrap intervals of this kind may not
fully capture the true sampling uncertainty; the intervals below should be
read as indicative of the scale of uncertainty, not as precise bounds.
Channel identity is recorded for this corpus (Section~\ref{sec:experimentalsetup});
Appendix~\ref{app:channelci} reports these same intervals resampled by
channel rather than by transcript, since several channels contribute more
than one transcript to this 30-item sample.

moderate-to-good level (AC1\,=\,0.63) and agree with CRUCIBLE's label 90\%
of the time. This is consistent with this domain's fast, stable
convergence during calibration (Table~\ref{tab:results}: 96.7\% agreement,
v0 rubric, no revisions) and is consistent with convergence here
reflecting genuine label validity, not shared model artifact, for this
annotator group.

\textbf{Technology \& AI: this domain's annotator group's judgments
diverged from CRUCIBLE despite agreeing with each other.} These annotators
agree with each other at the same rate as General Education's annotators
(AC1\,=\,0.63)---for this group, the task was well-posed and answerable
consistently. Yet their consensus matches CRUCIBLE's label only 50\% of
the time. Because human-human agreement is high while human-CRUCIBLE
agreement is low, this is not consistent with an inherently ambiguous task
for this annotator group; it is evidence that CRUCIBLE's confident
convergence (81.7\% inter-judge agreement, crossing our calibration
threshold) \emph{masked} a systematic divergence from this group's
judgment---a known failure mode in LLM-as-judge pipelines: high automated
agreement gives no signal that labels are unreliable when the automated
panel and the human reference diverge in a way agreement metrics alone
cannot detect, and Technology \& AI's agreement statistics here are
empirically indistinguishable from General Education's. These annotators
were screened for English proficiency and Prolific approval rating, not
for technical or AI/ML domain expertise, which may itself contribute to
the divergence in ways we cannot separate from a genuine CRUCIBLE
misalignment with this group's judgment.

\textbf{Career \& Self-Improvement: this domain's annotator group itself
showed low agreement.} These annotators show markedly lower agreement with
each other (AC1\,=\,0.33) than the other two groups. Since this group
could not consistently agree among themselves, CRUCIBLE's own stagnation
here (Table~\ref{tab:results}: 68.4\%--70.0\%, never crossing threshold)
is at least partly attributable to genuine task difficulty for this group
rather than model failure alone. This nuances---without fully
resolving---our capability-ceiling diagnosis (Section~\ref{sec:cotresults}
tests this directly): the ceiling may be substantially a property of the
content itself as judged by this group, not solely the judge models.

\subsubsection{Implications}

These results require us to revise the framing of CRUCIBLE's central claim.
Automated inter-judge convergence is \emph{not sufficient evidence of
alignment with human judgment}: it correctly identified General
Education's annotator group as reliable and correctly flagged Career \&
Self-Improvement's as difficult, but it failed to distinguish Technology \&
AI's unreliable convergence from General Education's valid convergence,
despite near-identical agreement statistics. We therefore do not claim
CRUCIBLE eliminates the need for human oversight; we show instead that
periodic, targeted human validation---at the scale used here, a small
stratified sample per domain---is advisable, and appears necessary before
treating automated agreement alone as evidence of validity in settings
similar to this one, to determine \emph{which} domains' automated
convergence can be trusted, a determination the convergence metric alone
cannot make.

We did not recalibrate CRUCIBLE's rubrics toward the human labels obtained
here. Doing so would reintroduce exactly the circularity this validation
study is designed to break: a rubric tuned to match a specific human sample
provides no evidence of generalization beyond that sample. The role of
this validation is diagnostic, not corrective.

in our data. Task descriptions accurately represented the nature of the

\subsection{Rubric Portability Across Judge Models}
\label{sec:portability}

A natural question is why a single frontier model, applied zero-shot,
would not suffice in place of the full calibration loop. We test this
directly, and in doing so uncover a second finding: the calibrated rubric
does not transfer cleanly to a judge model different from the one it was
calibrated on.

\subsubsection{Frontier Baseline Design}

We compare three approaches against the same 30-transcript human ground
truth used in Section~\ref{sec:humanval}: (1)~the local three-judge panel
applying its calibrated rubric (the system described in this paper);
(2)~GPT-5.6~Sol~\cite{openaiGPT56Sol} (OpenAI, general availability July 9, 2026) applying a
naive zero-shot prompt with no rubric; and (3)~GPT-5.6~Sol applying the
same calibrated rubric used by the local panel. All GPT-5.6~Sol calls for
this experiment were made on July 19, 2026, ten days after general
availability. We use a non-Anthropic model for this
baseline because Claude serves exclusively as the calibration coordinator
throughout this work and was not evaluated as a judge, to avoid conflating
baseline performance with the model family responsible for rubric design.
GPT-5.6~Sol does not support temperature\,=\,0 at time of writing; results
reflect the model's default sampling temperature.

\subsubsection{Findings}

Overall, the local calibrated panel and a single zero-shot frontier pass
achieve identical accuracy against human judgment (63.3\%)---the 10-iteration
calibration loop does not categorically outperform a single frontier
zero-shot pass in aggregate. However, giving the frontier model the
\emph{same} calibrated rubric text does not add value, and in Career \&
Self-Improvement, degrades accuracy substantially (60\%\,$\to$\,20\%
against human judgment). Comparing the same frontier model's outputs
against the local panel's labels directly (rather than against human
judgment) shows the rubric \emph{does} make GPT-5.6~Sol's outputs converge
toward the local panel's specific behavior (agreement with the local panel
rises from 0.500 to 0.633 when the rubric is added). Taken together, this
indicates the calibrated rubric teaches a frontier judge to mimic the local
ensemble's particular behavior pattern---including the local ensemble's
specific errors---rather than making the frontier judge more accurate
against the available human reference. The rubric appears co-adapted to the specific quirks
of the local judge ensemble it was calibrated on (Section~\ref{sec:results},
which documents Qwen~2.5~7B's specific misreadings that motivated each
revision), and this adaptation does not generalize to a different judge
model.

We verified this baseline is not a single-draw artifact: repeating both the
no-rubric and rubric-prompted GPT-5.6~Sol calls five times per transcript
(600 total calls) shows 87--93\% of transcripts receive an identical label
across all five draws, and the majority-of-five accuracy against the
available human reference matches the original single-draw result exactly
for both variants.

\subsubsection{Reasoning-Before-Label Ablation}
\label{sec:cotresults}

Motivated by the question of whether Career \&
Self-Improvement's stagnation (Table~\ref{tab:results}) reflects a
single-pass prompting limitation rather than a true model capability
ceiling, we added an intermediate reasoning step to the local judges'
prompts, in the style of chain-of-thought
prompting~\cite{wei2022chainofthought} (reasoning generated before the
label, not after), and re-scored all three domains against their existing
calibrated rubrics, using the same three local models at
temperature\,=\,0.0. We call this a \emph{reasoning-before-label prompt}
rather than chain-of-thought throughout, since we have no access to the
models' actual internal computation---only to text generated before the
label in response to an instruction to reason first.

The reasoning step reliably raises inter-judge agreement in the two
domains that had room to improve (Career: +8.3 points weighted; Technology
\& AI: +11.6 points, Gwet's AC1 nearly triples), but it never \emph{helps},
and in Career actively \emph{hurts}, agreement with human judgment.
Career's human-agreement drops from 50\% to 40\% with the reasoning step
(Cohen's $\kappa$ flips from $+0.14$ to $-0.25$); Technology \& AI is flat
on raw agreement but its AC1 drops; General Education, already
near-saturated, is unchanged against humans and slightly dips on
inter-judge agreement, consistent with a ceiling effect.

This result rules out simple single-pass prompting failure as the sole
explanation for Career \& Self-Improvement's stagnation---the judges
\emph{can} be made to agree more with reasoning added---but shows that
forcing reasoning-before-label makes the panel more internally consistent
without making that consistency more correct. We revise our diagnosis
accordingly: the original stagnation reflects not an inability of the
judges to converge, but a shared misalignment between judge and human
notions of ``substantive'' career content, one that explicit reasoning
reinforces rather than resolves. We used a single, untuned
reasoning-before-label prompt design across all domains; whether a more
carefully engineered prompt would alter this pattern is untested and left
for future work.

\subsubsection{Revision-Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:revisionsensitivity}

The findings above raise a further question: how much of the
coordinator's v1 revision's effect (Technology \& AI, 76.7\%\,$\to$\,81.7\%)
is attributable to the coordinator's specific diagnostic reasoning, versus
simply having \emph{any} reasonable revision informed by the disagreement
evidence, versus having any revision at all? We call this a
\emph{revision-sensitivity analysis}, not an ablation: a single alternative
revision per condition cannot isolate the coordinator's causal
contribution, only indicate whether the pattern is sensitive to how the
revision is produced.

We regenerated the v0-iteration disagreement cases for Technology \& AI's
20 transcripts via a fresh deterministic pass (temperature\,=\,0.0; these
were not persisted from the original run, so this is how they are
recovered) and generated two alternative revisions, both via
GPT-5.6~Sol\footnote{No Anthropic API access was available for this
analysis; both alternatives substitute GPT-5.6~Sol for Claude Sonnet, the
original coordinator model. This is a genuine confound: differences from
the original coordinator's v1 rubric may partly reflect model-family
differences in rubric-writing style rather than purely the evidence-visibility
manipulation this analysis targets.}: (a)~\emph{disagreement-informed}, seeing
the same disagreement cases and criterion quotes the original coordinator
saw, but not the coordinator's own revision text; and (b)~\emph{rubric-only},
seeing only the v0 rubric with no disagreement evidence at all. Both
alternatives were run through the same local three-judge panel on the same
20 transcripts, single inference pass, no further iteration.

In this single trial, the original coordinator outperformed both
alternatives, and having disagreement evidence was not by itself
sufficient: alt (a), given the \emph{same} evidence the coordinator saw,
performed \emph{worse} than alt (b)'s blind, no-evidence revision, which in
turn was statistically indistinguishable from not revising at all. Looking
at the generated text, alt (a) rewrote multiple HIGH criteria broadly and
more restrictively, where the original coordinator made a single surgical
clause edit (Appendix~\ref{app:rubrics}); the broad rewrite appears to have
destabilized rather than fixed the panel. We do not read this as
establishing that the coordinator's diagnostic reasoning is necessary or
superior in general: both alternatives were generated by a different model
than the original coordinator, so this comparison cannot cleanly separate
``the coordinator's reasoning is better'' from ``Claude Sonnet writes
better surgical rubric edits than GPT-5.6~Sol for this task.'' We report it
as a sensitivity finding, not a controlled ablation result.

These findings---rubric non-transfer across judge models, a
reasoning-before-label step increasing agreement without increasing
agreement with the available human reference, the frontier ensemble tracking human judgment about as
loosely as the local panel (Section~\ref{sec:frontierhuman}), and the
Section~\ref{sec:humanval} finding that Technology \& AI's confident
convergence masked a divergence from human judgment---share a common
implication demonstrated several ways: \textbf{making automated judges
agree more with each other is not the same as making them more correct.}
Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement} adds a fifth, more direct demonstration
of the same point via judge replacement. We note the human-validation,
rubric-portability, and reasoning-before-label analyses in this section
share a common 30-item human-validation reference set (n\,=\,30); while
each tests a distinct manipulation, they should be read as related
diagnostic analyses on one reference set, not as independent replications,
since a property specific to this particular set could in principle affect
several of them jointly.

\subsection{What CRUCIBLE Addresses}

CRUCIBLE substitutes
structured inter-judge disagreement---measurable, diagnosable, and
actionable---for the implicit and unauditable disagreement that characterizes
single-annotator labeling.

This is not a claim that ensemble labels are unbiased. Each judge carries its
own training biases. But the ensemble structure makes those biases visible:
when one model systematically diverges from the other two, the coordinator can
name the pattern. Human annotator bias is usually invisible unless multiple
annotators are compared---precisely the expensive intervention this system
replaces.

\subsection{The Capability Ceiling Finding}
\label{sec:ceiling}

The Career \& Self-Improvement ceiling---a best of 70.0\%, short of
convergence---shows that a class of disagreement exists that rubric revision did not fix under
this judge and prompt setup. Without the CRUCIBLE diagnostic loop, an
operator seeing 70.0\% would not know whether to keep revising or replace
the judge. The stagnation pattern plus the coordinator's consistent
diagnosis across both revision attempts---attributing it to a model-level
inability to track co-occurrence patterns across long documents---suggested
the rubric was not the limiting factor. The natural prescription is judge
replacement or ensemble augmentation, not further revision;
Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement} tests this directly, though as that
section shows in detail, the specific test we were able to run cannot
fully distinguish a genuine judge-level capability limitation from a
structural property of this particular three-judge panel.

\subsection{Judge Replacement: A Direct Test}
\label{sec:judgereplacement}

Section~\ref{sec:ceiling} recommends judge replacement, not further rubric
revision, once a domain is diagnosed as capability-bounded. We test this
directly on Career \& Self-Improvement, replacing Qwen~2.5~7B---the judge
the coordinator's diagnosis consistently implicated---with
\texttt{granite3.1-dense:8b} (IBM, 8.2B parameters, 131{,}072-token
context), keeping Llama~3.1~8B and Mistral~7B unchanged and the retained
v1 rubric fixed. This is the only replacement model attempted: we selected
it before running any evaluation, for comparable scale to Qwen~2.5~7B and a
training lineage distinct from Meta, Mistral AI, and Alibaba---explicitly
not Gemma~2~9B, which Section~\ref{sec:judgeqc} already disqualified as
degenerate. Trying multiple candidates and reporting the best-performing
one would constitute model selection on the same small human-reference set
used to score it below, which we specifically avoided.

\footnotesize

\textbf{Panel} & \textbf{Inter-judge $A$} & \textbf{vs.\ Human} & \textbf{vs.\ Human ($\kappa$ / AC1)} \\

Replacing the diagnosed-bottleneck judge raises inter-judge agreement
substantially, from 0.700 to 0.883. Accuracy against the 10 human-labeled
Career transcripts is \emph{bit-for-bit identical} before and after: the
replacement panel's majority-vote label matches the original panel's on
every one of the 10 items, so agreement, Cohen's $\kappa$, and Gwet's AC1
against human judgment are unchanged to three decimal places.

\textbf{Why: a mechanical check on the full 20-transcript set.} We looked
more closely at why, using the full 20-transcript Career set (not just the
10 with human labels), and found this dissociation is mechanically
guaranteed rather than an independently interesting empirical outcome.
Llama~3.1~8B and Mistral~7B---the two judges held fixed across both
panels---already agree with each other on 18 of the 20 (90\%) Career
transcripts (Llama predicts HIGH on 20/20, Mistral on 18/20); on those 18,
the majority-vote label is determined entirely by Llama and Mistral,
regardless of what the third judge outputs. On the remaining 2
transcripts (Llama\,=\,HIGH, Mistral\,=\,LOW), both the original judge
(Qwen~2.5~7B, 10\% HIGH rate on this domain) and the replacement
(granite3.1-dense, 65\% HIGH rate) happened to vote LOW, so the majority
stayed LOW under both panels there too. The result: the majority-vote
label changed on \textbf{0 of the 20} Career transcripts between the
original and replacement panel. The inter-judge agreement metric moved
(0.700\,$\to$\,0.883) because granite3.1-dense agrees with the
Llama+Mistral majority far more often than Qwen did (65\% vs.\ 10\% HIGH
rate), converting many 2-1 splits into 3-0 unanimity---not because the
panel's actual output changed on any transcript.

This means the finding is narrower than the table alone suggests: what we
have directly demonstrated is that judge replacement changed whether the
panel was unanimous or split, not what the panel's actual annotations
were, and by construction the annotations \emph{could not} have changed on
this particular panel composition. This result is \emph{consistent
with}---but does not independently establish---this paper's broader claim
that fixing inter-judge agreement does not fix agreement with human
judgment: the majority-vote label here was mechanically unchangeable by
any third judge given how strongly the other two already agreed, so the
null result on human alignment cannot be attributed with confidence to
\emph{correcting} a genuine capability gap versus simply \emph{being
unable to move} the panel's output at all. The label prevalence underlying
this is informative too: both panels produce the same distribution of
HIGH/LOW calls at the majority-vote level on this subset, which is
consistent with the two panels reaching the same place by different
within-panel routes rather than the replacement judge correcting Qwen's
specific misreadings---though, per the analysis above, ``reaching the same
place'' was close to inevitable given the fixed pair's own agreement rate.

We read this cautiously in two respects: it is a single replacement-model
trial on one domain (see Limitations), and, as detailed above, the extent
to which any third judge could have influenced the majority-vote outcome
at all is itself limited on this panel. This experiment is better read as
evidence that judge replacement can convert a split panel into a unanimous
one without changing its output, rather than as a demonstration that judge
replacement is a general recovery strategy for capability-bounded
disagreement (see Limitations for a fuller discussion of this
constraint).

\subsection{Degenerate Judge Detection}

A judge assigning a single label to all documents makes agreement appear high
when it is driven by shared degeneracy rather than shared understanding.
We treat discriminative validity as a pre-screening gate: no model enters
a judge panel without demonstrating a non-degenerate label distribution across
a diagnostic sample.

\subsection{Output-Stability Recheck for Converged Domains}
\label{sec:regressioncheck}

Once a domain converges, we re-evaluate it with the same rubric and the
same three judge models on the iteration immediately following convergence,
checking whether the weighted agreement rate holds. Each coordinator call
is a fresh, stateless API request scoped to a single domain (it receives
only that domain's rubric, disagreement cases, and revision history; no
information about other domains' prompts, revisions, or outputs is ever
included), so this check is not, and cannot be, a defense against one
domain's revision ``priming'' the coordinator's phrasing on another domain
via shared state---no such state exists in this implementation. What it
does test is judge-level output stability: whether the same rubric applied
to the same transcripts by the same three models reproduces the same
agreement rate on a second pass, since a frontier API coordinator's own
non-determinism (Limitations) is a property of the coordinator, not the
judges, but serving-side variance in the local judge models is not fully
ruled out either. No regression (agreement drop $>10$ percentage points)
was observed in the run reported here across any of the domains checked.

% ── 7. Limitations ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Single experiment.} Results are for 60 transcripts across three
domains. Generalization requires validation on other domains and document types.

\textbf{Model-specific ceiling.} The Career stagnation was diagnosed as specific to Qwen~2.5~7B under our prompt and setup; we
cannot rule out that a different prompt formulation or judge model would shift the ceiling.
Future work should evaluate judge replacement and prompt variation as recovery strategies.
This attribution rests on a single, untuned prompt template per judge model
used throughout the calibration run reported here; prompt reformulation
remains untested as an alternative explanation for the ceiling and should be
evaluated alongside judge replacement before concluding the ceiling is
inherent to the model rather than an artifact of this specific prompt.

\textbf{Judge replacement: tested once, narrowly.} Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}
now tests Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}'s recommendation directly: replacing
Qwen~2.5~7B with granite3.1-dense:8b on Career \& Self-Improvement raised
inter-judge agreement substantially (0.700\,$\to$\,0.883) while leaving
accuracy against the 10 human-labeled transcripts completely unchanged.
This is a single replacement-model trial on a single domain, with only one
candidate model attempted; it does not establish that judge replacement is
a general recovery strategy.

\textbf{Judge replacement's strength depends on how much the third judge
could actually influence the outcome.} As Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}
details, the two retained judges (Llama, Mistral) already agreed with each
other on 90\% of this domain's 20 transcripts, and on the remaining 2 both
candidate third judges happened to vote the same way; the majority-vote
label therefore changed on 0 of 20 transcripts between the original and
replacement panel. This means the experiment, as run, could only ever have
shown a dissociation between inter-judge agreement and human alignment on
this domain---it was structurally incapable of showing the opposite,
because the panel's output could not move. A domain or panel where the two
retained judges disagree with each other more often (so the third judge's
identity genuinely determines more majority votes) would be a stronger,
more informative test of whether judge replacement recovers human
alignment along with inter-judge agreement; we did not have such a case
available in this run's data. Readers should weight this result
accordingly: it demonstrates a real property of the diagnostic loop
(replacement resolved the agreement symptom) but the null result on human
alignment is consistent with, rather than independent confirmation of,
this paper's broader claim.

\textbf{Silver labels.} Calibrated labels retain the biases of the three judge
models. Section~\ref{sec:humanval}'s human validation study addresses part of
this---human majority consensus matches CRUCIBLE's calibrated label 90\%
of the time in General Education but only 50\% in Technology \& AI and
Career \& Self-Improvement---but that study itself uses a small
($n=10$/domain) unaided-judgment human sample (see below), not a
large-scale or rubric-guided gold standard. For high-stakes tasks, human
adjudication of remaining disagreements at greater scale is recommended.

\textbf{Synthetic data in classifier training.} Of the 389 transcripts used
to train the downstream SVM classifier (Section~\ref{sec:results}), 300
(77\%) are synthetically generated with labels assigned by construction,
intended to match the generating prompt's target class, rather than
assigned by the calibrated-rubric ensemble. The reported
F1\,=\,0.871 may therefore partly reflect the classifier separating
synthetic-generation artifacts from real transcripts, rather than purely
the quality of the calibrated real-transcript labels. A real-transcript-only
training ablation would be needed to isolate how much of the reported F1 is
attributable to the calibrated labels specifically.

\textbf{Coordinator dependency.} Revision quality depends on the coordinator
model. A weaker coordinator may misdiagnose disagreement or introduce new
ambiguities. Retry logic mitigates transient failures but not systematic ones.
Because the coordinator is a frontier API model and is not bit-reproducible across runs, we
expect the exact per-domain ceiling to vary by roughly one to two points between runs, based on
typical run-to-run variance for this kind of model; we have not verified this band empirically,
since, as noted immediately below, only a single calibration run was performed. We accordingly
do \emph{not} claim the qualitative findings (the capability ceiling, degenerate-judge detection,
and the three agreement regimes) are empirically robust to run-to-run variation---only that they
are unlikely to be artifacts of a single anomalous coordinator call, given that each rests on a
consistent diagnosis repeated across multiple iterations within the one run we did perform.

\textbf{Single calibration run.} The reported convergence trajectory
(Table~\ref{tab:trajectory}) reflects a single run of a non-deterministic
coordinator, and no repeated-run robustness check was performed. In particular, Career \& Self-Improvement's iteration-2 gain
(68.4\%\,$\to$\,70.0\%, 1.6 points) falls within the estimated $\pm$1--2 point
run-to-run variance band described above; that specific
incremental result should therefore be read cautiously as a possible
artifact of coordinator noise rather than confirmed as a genuine
improvement from the rubric revision. Repeated runs would be needed to
establish it as signal rather than noise, and more generally to establish
empirically (rather than argue plausibly) that this paper's qualitative
findings are robust to coordinator variation.

\textbf{No baseline comparison, partially addressed.} We did not benchmark
CRUCIBLE against manual rubric revision or simple self-refinement; these
remain future work. A single frontier judge \emph{is} now compared, both
without a rubric and with the calibrated rubric
(Section~\ref{sec:portability}), so that specific gap is closed. Isolating
the coordinator-mediated loop's contribution from these still-untested
baselines remains open.

\textbf{Coordinator contribution: sensitivity-tested, not isolated.}
Section~\ref{sec:revisionsensitivity} now compares the original coordinator's
revision against two alternatives on Technology \& AI---one given the same
disagreement evidence, one given only the rubric---and finds the original
coordinator outperformed both, while the disagreement-informed alternative
underperformed the blind one. This is informative but does not isolate the
coordinator's causal contribution: both alternatives were generated by a
different model (GPT-5.6~Sol, substituting for the unavailable Claude
Sonnet coordinator), so the comparison cannot cleanly separate ``the
coordinator's diagnostic reasoning is better'' from ``Claude Sonnet writes
better surgical rubric edits than GPT-5.6~Sol for this task,'' and only one
alternative per condition was tried on one domain. A same-model ablation
comparing coordinator-proposed revisions against alternative revisions of
similar scope---ideally using Claude Sonnet for all conditions, or adding
human-written alternatives---would more cleanly isolate whether the
coordinator's specific diagnosis matters. This remains future work.

\textbf{Small human-validation sample.} Our human validation
(Section~\ref{sec:humanval}) uses $n=10$ real transcripts per domain, plus
one attention check, a scale chosen as a practical minimum
sufficient for this purpose. With $n=10$ per domain, the percentile
bootstrap intervals in Table~\ref{tab:humanval} may not fully capture the
true sampling uncertainty and should be read as indicative rather than
precise; they are also wide (Technology \& AI's and Career \&
Self-Improvement's overlap substantially), and our domain-level
distinction rests on the joint pattern of human-human and human-model
agreement, not on point estimates alone. Channel identity is recorded for
this corpus, and resampling by channel rather than by transcript
(Appendix~\ref{app:channelci}) changes these intervals only slightly
(overall upper bound 80.0\%\,$\to$\,79.3\%), since most channels contribute
only one transcript to this particular 30-item sample---but the underlying
60-transcript corpus does have repeated channels (up to 5 transcripts from
one channel), so this should not be read as ruling out channel-level
dependence in general, only as showing it is small for this specific
sample. A larger-scale replication---human evaluation across the full
60-transcript corpus---would narrow these intervals and is scoped as
future work. Relatedly, for Career \& Self-Improvement specifically (human
AC1\,=\,0.33), the 2-of-3 majority vote used as ``human consensus''
throughout Sections~\ref{sec:humanval},~\ref{sec:frontierhuman},
and~\ref{sec:portability} is itself a noisy estimate given how weakly the
three annotators agree with each other on this domain; annotator-level
uncertainty is not propagated into the human-vs-CRUCIBLE comparison
(Table~\ref{tab:humanval}) or the frontier-baseline comparison
(Table~\ref{tab:frontier}), both of which treat the majority label as a
fixed point estimate. Separately, our human task uses an unaided-judgment
design only (Section~\ref{sec:humanval}'s Design subsection): annotators
were never shown the calibrated rubric. This tests whether CRUCIBLE's
labels align with unaided human intuition, not whether humans agree with
the rubric's specific operational criteria when shown it; a two-condition
design (unaided plus rubric-guided) would additionally test construct
validity of the rubric's criteria themselves and is scoped as future work.

\textbf{Held-out generalization is narrower than it may sound.}
Section~\ref{sec:heldout}'s positive result generalizes the rubric
revision's effect on \emph{inter-judge agreement}, not on agreement with a
human reference (no human labels exist for that 90-transcript corpus), and
the ``unseen-to-coordinator'' claim rests on documented project workflow,
not a logged, timestamped audit trail ruling out the corpus's exposure
during development. The two corpora also share 6 channels
(Section~\ref{sec:experimentalsetup}), so this is document-held-out, not
source-held-out. Most materially, the effect is driven disproportionately
by one judge (Qwen~2.5~7B) shifting its label distribution toward the
other two, largely static, judges---not by uniform three-way convergence
toward more nuanced shared judgment---and the underlying majority-vote
annotation changes on \textbf{0 of the 90} held-out transcripts between
the v0 and final rubric (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}), so the generalization
result should not be read as evidence the calibrated rubric produces more
broadly reliable judgment on unseen data, or that it changes any actual
output label on this set, only that it reliably shifts one judge's
individual predictions in a direction that happens not to move the
already-fixed majority.

\textbf{Frontier baseline sampling.} GPT-5.6~Sol does not expose temperature
control; our stability check (Section~\ref{sec:portability}) characterizes
label stability under the model's default sampling rather than variance at
a fixed, controlled temperature, and cannot separate genuine sampling noise
from serving-side non-determinism.

\textbf{Reasoning-before-label scope.} The reasoning-before-label ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:cotresults}) uses a single, untuned prompt design tested
across all three domains but not across alternative formulations; whether
more carefully engineered reasoning prompts alter the pattern of raised
inter-judge agreement without raised agreement with the human reference is
untested.

\textbf{Shared evaluation reference.} The human validation, frontier-vs-human
comparison, rubric-portability, reasoning-before-label, and the
human-alignment portion of the judge-replacement analysis
(Sections~\ref{sec:humanval},~\ref{sec:frontierhuman},~\ref{sec:portability},~\ref{sec:cotresults},
and~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}) are all evaluated against the same
30-transcript human-validation set (or, for judge replacement, its Career
subset). They test distinct manipulations but are related diagnostic
analyses on one reference set, not independent replications in the
statistical sense: a property specific to this particular reference set
could in principle affect several of them jointly. The revision-sensitivity
analysis (Section~\ref{sec:revisionsensitivity}) and held-out evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:heldout}) use different corpora and are not subject to
this specific concern, though each has its own scope limits noted above.

\textbf{Reproducibility of the original run's exact configuration.} Before
treating anything as unrecoverable, we checked available sources for the
primary calibration run's exact configuration. Recovered: the run's
timestamp (2026-05-31, from \texttt{calibration\_summary.json} and
corroborated by output file modification times); current digests,
quantization (Q4\_K\_M for all three original judges), and context window
(32{,}768 tokens for Qwen~2.5~7B and Mistral~7B, 131{,}072 for
Llama~3.1~8B) via \texttt{ollama show}, which local file modification
timestamps (``7 weeks'' prior to this writing) are consistent with, but do
not prove, matching what was used in the original run; and confirmation
from the judge-calling code itself (\texttt{judge\_agent.py}) that
temperature was explicitly set to 0.0 and no context-window override was
applied, so each model's default context window applied throughout---ample
for this task's prompts (transcripts capped at 3{,}000 characters plus a
short rubric, well under any of these limits), which rules out
context-window truncation as an explanation for Qwen's specific
misreadings, addressing one previously open question. Not recoverable:
shell history containing the original \texttt{ollama pull} commands and
their exact timestamps (none found); API billing or usage logs for the
Claude Sonnet coordinator calls (not accessible in this environment); and,
most importantly, a saved digest snapshot from \emph{the original run
itself} to diff against today's digests, since none was captured at the
time. Follow-up experiments in this paper (Sections~\ref{sec:heldout},~\ref{sec:judgereplacement},
and~\ref{sec:revisionsensitivity}) were conducted under this closest
recoverable configuration and should be interpreted as diagnostic
extensions rather than exact controlled replications of the original run.

\textbf{Runtime.} At 17--20 minutes per iteration, a 10-iteration run requires
nearly three hours. Parallelizing judge evaluation across models would reduce
iteration time by roughly two-thirds.

% ── 8. Conclusion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

\looseness=-1
CRUCIBLE demonstrates that annotation rubric calibration can be automated, after an initial rubric draft,
through a coordinator-mediated iterative loop driven by inter-judge
agreement signal, with no human reference labels used during optimization
and no human in the loop after initialization (every retention, rollback,
irreducibility-flagging, and stopping decision in the loop is programmatic;
see Section~\ref{sec:calibrationloop}). The
system was built to solve a concrete problem---an earlier, differently-scoped
pilot showed inconsistent, largely-uncalibrated judge disagreement
(Introduction, Appendix~\ref{app:baseline})---and raw weighted agreement
using the local calibration panel reached 81.7\%--96.7\% on two domains,
though chance-corrected agreement shows the genuine gain concentrates in
Technology \& AI. This paper's central finding, however, is not that
number: it is that raising or achieving inter-judge agreement is not the
same as achieving agreement with human judgment, demonstrated five ways
(Sections~\ref{sec:humanval},~\ref{sec:frontierhuman},~\ref{sec:portability},~\ref{sec:cotresults},
and~\ref{sec:judgereplacement})---most directly by judge replacement, which
raised inter-judge agreement from 0.700 to 0.883 while leaving human
agreement bit-for-bit unchanged. As Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement}
details, that specific result is narrower than it first appears: the two
retained judges already agreed with each other on 90\% of the domain's
transcripts, so the panel's majority-vote output was mechanically
unchangeable by any third judge, and the null result on human alignment is
consistent with, rather than independent proof of, the broader claim. The
framework's durable value is its diagnosis of disagreement structure, a
check that this diagnosis's effect on the inter-judge \emph{agreement
metric} generalizes past the calibration corpus even though the underlying
majority-vote annotations do not change on the held-out set
(Section~\ref{sec:heldout}), and the downstream classifier case study
(F1\,=\,0.871) the calibrated rubrics enabled.

\looseness=-1
Beyond the specific numbers, CRUCIBLE surfaces one finding with broader
implications: the capability ceiling, and its limits. Stagnation under
coordinator-mediated revision is a diagnostic---it identifies that the
limiting factor is plausibly related to a judge's model-level capability
rather than rubric underspecification, though our judge-replacement test
cannot cleanly separate a genuine capability limitation from a structural
property of a panel where two judges already agree most of the time, and
motivates judge replacement or ensemble augmentation as directions worth
testing over further revision. Section~\ref{sec:judgereplacement} tests
judge replacement directly and shows it resolves the agreement symptom
completely without changing the panel's output at all, on the one domain
and one replacement model tested, and by construction could not have
changed the output on this specific panel---a result that sharpens rather
than closes the diagnostic question. On a practical note, the columnar
evaluation order (batching all transcripts per model before switching)
reduced per-iteration model-load events from 180 to 3, making a full
10-iteration run tractable on an Apple Silicon MacBook with no discrete
GPU (exact CPU/GPU offload configuration for the Ollama-served models was
not recorded).

Code, calibration logs, versioned rubrics, and calibrated labels are publicly
available at \url{https://github.com/biditdas18/rubric-calibration-agent}.

\section{Rubric Calibration: Before and After}
\label{app:rubrics}

We show the initial (v0) and final calibrated rubric text for each domain.
Only HIGH-signal criteria are shown for brevity; full rubrics (including
LOW-signal criteria and conflict rules) are available in the released
\texttt{calibrated\_rubrics.json}.

\textbf{General Education (v0 $\to$ v0, unchanged).} General Education
converged on iteration~1 with zero rubric changes; the initial rubric
required no revision.

\textbf{Technology \& AI (v0 $\to$ v1).}
\textit{v0:} ``Names specific technologies, algorithms, libraries, or
architectural patterns with accurate technical detail (e.g.\ `use Redis for
session caching', `transformer attention is $O(n^2)$ in sequence
length')''.
\textit{v1 (retained):} ``Names specific technologies, algorithms, data
structures, or architectural patterns with accurate technical detail (e.g.\
stack-based $O(n)$ solution, Project Mainline module updates, M5 neural
engine)''. The revision broadened ``libraries'' to ``data structures'' and
replaced software-engineering-specific examples with domain-neutral
technical examples, addressing Qwen~2.5~7B's overly narrow reading of the
original criterion; this single change raised agreement from 76.7\% to
81.7\%.

\textbf{Career \& Self-Improvement (v0 $\to$ v1 retained, v2 discarded).}
\textit{v0:} ``Contains at least 2 specific procedural steps with named
methods, tools, or timelines (e.g.\ `File Form I-539 within 60 days', `Use
the STAR method for interviews')''.
\textit{v1 (retained as final):} ``Contains at least 2 specific procedural
steps with named methods, tools, timelines, or defined techniques---conceptual
definitions alone do not qualify but named practices or described processes
do''. This revision added an explicit exclusion clause to address
Qwen~2.5~7B crediting abstract restatement as procedural content, raising
weighted agreement from 68.4\% to 70.0\%---the domain's best-observed
result. A second revision (v2, not reproduced here as it was not retained)
further tightened the co-occurrence condition between motivational language
and procedural content; this regressed agreement to 68.4\% and was
discarded in favor of v1. The revisions shown above increase specificity
rather than simplify toward keyword-matching, addressing the concern that
rubric calibration might optimize for consensus by trivializing criteria
rather than sharpening them.

\section{3-Category Pilot Baseline: Full Figures}
\label{app:baseline}

During development, an earlier, differently-scoped pilot annotation
pipeline used a domain-agnostic rubric applied across a frontier chat-model
ensemble (GPT-4, Gemini, Meta AI), reported in the companion SNR
paper~\cite{dasSNR2026}, Section~4.1. That pilot used a 3-category label
space (HIGH/MID/LOW) later collapsed to binary via majority vote for
downstream use, computed from the raw per-judge labels: 41.1\% average
pairwise agreement across the three judge pairs, and 16.7\% full three-way
unanimity (10/60 transcripts fully unanimous; 44/60 with exactly one
matching pair; 6/60 with all three judges giving different labels). The
6/60 fully-split rate is possible because the label space has 3 categories,
not 2 as in CRUCIBLE's own metric---we do not attempt to reconcile the
41.1\% and 16.7\% figures via a binary-case formula, since that formula
does not apply to a 3-category space; both figures are independently
correct as computed directly from the raw per-judge labels.

\textbf{Non-comparability.} This pilot (3-category label space, a
different model ensemble, pairwise/unanimity metrics) and CRUCIBLE's own
calibration metric (binary label space, the local judge panel, the
weighted-$A$ metric of Eq.~\ref{eq:agreement}) are measured in different
label spaces with different models and are \textbf{not} directly
comparable in magnitude. We present the pilot as qualitative motivating
context---an independently-run experiment showing low, inconsistent
agreement in an uncalibrated, differently-scoped setup---not as a
quantitative pre/post baseline for CRUCIBLE's own reported convergence
numbers (76.7\%--96.7\% weighted $A$). No percentage-point improvement
between the pilot and CRUCIBLE's results is computed or implied anywhere
in this paper.

\section{Columnar Evaluation Order: Full Derivation}
\label{app:columnar}

The naive \emph{row-wise} approach:
\[
  T_1 \to [\text{Llama, Mistral, Qwen}] \to
  T_2 \to [\text{Llama, Mistral, Qwen}] \to \cdots
\]
requires Ollama to load, run, and unload three model weight sets for every
transcript. On consumer hardware with limited VRAM, each model switch
incurs a cold-load penalty. Across 60 transcripts and 3 models per
transcript, this produces 180 model-load events per iteration. In
practice, this scheme could not complete a single iteration within 30
minutes and was cancelled.

The fix draws on a principle from columnar database design: group
operations by column (model) rather than by row (transcript):
\[
  \underbrace{\text{Llama} \to 60\text{ transcripts}}_{\text{col.\,1}}
  \;\to\;
  \underbrace{\text{Mistral} \to 60\text{ transcripts}}_{\text{col.\,2}}
  \;\to\;
  \underbrace{\text{Qwen} \to 60\text{ transcripts}}_{\text{col.\,3}}
\]
This reduces model-load events from 180 to 3 total per iteration. Each
model loads once and remains resident in memory for its full 60-transcript
pass. Result: each iteration completed in 17--20 minutes on a consumer
MacBook (Apple Silicon, no discrete GPU; exact CPU/GPU offload
configuration not recorded), making a
10-iteration calibration run (170.7 minutes total; the run terminated at
the maximum-iteration cap of 10 iterations) fully tractable.

\section{Irreducible-Case Audit}
\label{app:irreducible}

\textbf{Independent audit: infeasible.} We attempted to independently
re-examine the coordinator's irreducible-case calls (Section~\ref{sec:results}
originally named two specific Technology \& AI transcripts) against the
actual per-case criterion quotes the coordinator saw. No such log survives:
inspection of \texttt{calibration\_loop.py} and \texttt{coordinator\_agent.py}
confirms only three files are ever persisted
(\texttt{calibrated\_rubrics.json}, \texttt{calibrated\_labels.csv},
\texttt{calibration\_summary.json}); flagged-irreducible transcript indices
are tracked only as an in-memory Python set during the run and never
written to disk. The specific transcript identities named in an earlier
draft of this paper could not be verified against any surviving artifact
and have been removed from the main text accordingly.

\textbf{Sensitivity check: completed.} Once a transcript is flagged
irreducible, \texttt{calibration\_loop.py} excludes it from that domain's
active evaluation pool for all subsequent iterations (confirmed by code
inspection), so it stops contributing to the domain's tracked weighted-$A$
denominator from that point on (the ``excluded'' treatment, i.e., the
number actually reported during the run). We compare this to an
``included'' treatment: the full re-relabeling of all 20 transcripts per
domain under the final rubric (\texttt{calibrated\_labels.csv}), which
counts every transcript regardless of irreducible-flag status. This is the
most direct recoverable proxy for ``included,'' not a reconstruction of
the original two flagged cases, since their specific identities are not
recoverable (above).

\section{Prolific Rejection Sensitivity Check}
\label{app:prolific}

One of nine human-validation submissions (Technology \& AI domain) was
rejected and replaced. Cross-referencing the pre- and post-replacement
raw batch exports against the officially recorded consensus
(\texttt{human\_validation\_ground\_truth.csv}) by testing which of the
four possible 3-of-4 annotator-response subsets exactly matches the
recorded consensus multiset on all 10 non-attention-check items identifies
the rejected annotator unambiguously (10/10 exact match for one subset;
6--7/10 for every other), independently confirmed against the Prolific
dashboard's Rejected tab (single rejected participant, matching the
rejected submission's internal record; completion time 39:06 against this
batch's 140:00 median).

Recomputing this domain's human consensus with the rejected annotator's
original responses reinstated in place of the replacement annotator's
changes \textbf{zero} of the 10 item-level consensus labels. Local-panel-vs-human
agreement for Technology \& AI is identical under both the official and
reinstated consensus (agreement 50.0\%, Cohen's $\kappa=-0.19$, Gwet's
AC1\,$=0.20$, both conditions). The domain-level conclusions reported in
Section~\ref{sec:humanval} do not depend on this exclusion.

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Career \& Self-Improvement's 10 sampled transcripts happen to come from 10
distinct channels, so its channel- and transcript-resampled intervals are
identical by construction. General Education and Technology \& AI each
have one repeated channel within their 10-item sample, producing a small

bound 80.0\%\,$\to$\,79.3\%). This should be read as evidence channel
clustering has a small effect \emph{on this particular 30-item sample},
not as evidence it would be negligible at different sample sizes or
samples with heavier channel repetition, such as the full 60-transcript
corpus (up to 5 transcripts from one channel) or the 90-transcript
training-supplement corpus (up to 7 from one channel).
